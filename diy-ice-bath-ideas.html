9 DIY & Budget Ice Bath Ideas for Cold Plunging at Home

Updated July 2026 · Research-based ideas by Plunge Picks · 7 min read

You don't need a $8,000 chiller tub to start cold plunging. Cold water is cold water — the physiological hit is the same whether it comes from a premium tub or a livestock trough full of ice. What a DIY or budget setup asks of you instead is a little effort: hauling ice, changing water, and accepting a less polished look. Below are nine practical ways people build a cold plunge cheaply, with honest pros, cons, and rough costs so you can pick the one that fits your space and budget.

Safety first. Any setup that mixes water with electricity — chest-freezer conversions especially — carries a real risk of electric shock. If a build involves a pump, chiller, or powered appliance, use a GFCI-protected outlet and, if you're not confident, get a qualified electrician. Cold immersion also isn't for everyone: if you have a heart condition, high blood pressure, or are pregnant, talk to a doctor first. See our medical disclaimer.

1. Galvanized stock tank — the classic

Galvanized stock tank ~$100–$250

A livestock watering trough is the most popular DIY plunge for a reason: it's cheap, big, and nearly indestructible. A 100–150 gallon tank costs around $100–$250 and lasts for years outdoors. The downside is zero insulation — ice melts fast — and an industrial look. Add a foam or pool-noodle lid to slow the melt.

Pros

  • Very cheap and extremely durable
  • Roomy — fits most body sizes
  • Easy to add a chiller later

Cons

  • No insulation — heavy ice use
  • Industrial appearance

2. Inflatable barrel tub

Inflatable barrel tub (e.g. The Cold Pod) Budget

Purpose-made inflatable tubs cost about as much as a nice dinner and pack away when not in use. They're insulated better than a bare trough, come with a lid, and are the easiest "no-tools" way to test whether cold plunging is for you before spending more.

Pros

  • Cheap and beginner-friendly
  • Packs away; portable
  • Some insulation + lid included

Cons

  • No temperature control (ice only)
  • Less durable than a tank

3. Large plastic tote or storage bin

Heavy-duty tote / storage bin ~$30–$80

A 50–100 gallon heavy-duty storage tote is the cheapest "tub-shaped" option. It works, but thin-walled bins can flex and crack under water pressure, so choose a rugged one and don't overfill. Best as a short-term way to try the habit before committing.

Pros

  • Extremely cheap and available anywhere
  • Light and easy to empty

Cons

  • Can flex or crack if flimsy
  • Often cramped for taller users

4. Repurposed bathtub (indoor or outdoor)

Old / freestanding bathtub Free–$300

If you already have a bathtub — or can find a used freestanding one — it's a ready-made plunge. Indoors you just fill and ice it; outdoors, a salvaged clawfoot tub makes a surprisingly nice garden plunge. Drainage and moving the tub are the main hassles.

Pros

  • May cost nothing if you already have one
  • Comfortable, familiar shape
  • Looks good outdoors

Cons

  • Heavy and awkward to move
  • Needs a drainage plan

5. IBC tote, cut down

Food-grade IBC tote (cut to size) ~$80–$200

A common DIY route: buy a food-grade IBC tote (the ~275-gallon cube in a metal cage), cut the top down to seat height, and you have a rigid, deep plunge with a built-in frame. Use a genuinely food-grade tote and clean it thoroughly first — you don't know what a used one previously held.

Pros

  • Deep and rigid with a built-in cage frame
  • Cheap for the size

Cons

  • Requires cutting and cleanup
  • Only buy food-grade; verify prior contents

6. Chest-freezer conversion — advanced, handle with care

Chest-freezer "cold plunge" ~$200–$500 + risk

A popular internet build turns a chest freezer into a self-cooling plunge. It's effective and cheap to run, but it puts water and mains electricity in close contact, which is a genuine shock hazard, and it can void warranties or invite mold if not sealed and maintained correctly. We're including it for completeness, not endorsing it: if you attempt it, treat electrical safety (GFCI, proper sealing) as non-negotiable and consider professional help. Many people are better served by a purpose-built tub.

Pros

  • Self-cooling — little to no ice
  • Cheap to run once built

Cons

  • Real electric-shock risk (water + mains)
  • Sealing, mold, and warranty issues

7. Stock tank + add a chiller later

Trough now, chiller upgrade later Staged

A sensible middle path: start with a cheap stock tank and ice, prove you'll stick with the habit, then add a standalone chiller and a simple pump loop to reach a no-ice setup — for far less than a premium all-in-one. Our tools can tell you whether and when a chiller pays for itself at your plunge frequency.

Pros

  • Low starting cost, clear upgrade path
  • Ends up cheaper than a premium tub

Cons

  • Two-step project
  • Chiller adds electrical setup

8. Insulated trough wrap

Any tub + insulation wrap +$20–$60

Not a tub itself, but the cheapest upgrade to any of the above: wrap the outside with foam board, a camping mat, or a pool cover, and add a floating lid. Better insulation means ice lasts far longer, which directly cuts your ongoing ice cost — often the biggest expense of a budget setup.

Pros

  • Cheap upgrade that cuts ice cost
  • Works on almost any tub

Cons

  • Adds bulk / a less sleek look

9. Kiddie pool — ultra-budget, short-term

Rigid kiddie / paddling pool ~$20–$60

The absolute cheapest entry point. A rigid-sided kiddie pool holds enough water to submerge to the waist or, in a bigger one, most of the body. It's not durable or insulated and looks like exactly what it is — but for a first week of trying cold water, it costs almost nothing.

Pros

  • Cheapest possible way to try it
  • Easy to store and empty

Cons

  • Not durable or insulated
  • Shallow — hard to fully submerge

Which DIY option should you pick?

If you just want to try cold plunging this week, start with a kiddie pool, a tote, or an inflatable tub. If you know you'll keep going and want something durable, a stock tank is the best value — and it leaves the door open to add a chiller later. Skip the chest-freezer build unless you're genuinely comfortable with the electrical safety involved.

Not sure ice is worth the hassle long-term? Run the numbers with our cost calculator and ice vs. chiller tool — for frequent plungers, a chiller can end up cheaper than a lifetime of bagged ice. If you'd rather skip DIY entirely, see our best budget cold plunge picks.
